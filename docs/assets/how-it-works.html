Bottle Vision

What Allen and I built on Saturday 10 Oct 2026, every piece of it, including the maths. Repo ~/dev/bottle-vision: 79 commits and about 5,000 lines of Python in roughly five hours. Each maths box has the formula, then what it means in plain words, then a worked example with real numbers from the repo. Anything marked DEEP is optional.
The 30-second version. One photo of a back bar goes in. Out comes every bottle with its brand, size and how full it is. We built two ways to do it. The first sends the photo to Claude and lets it answer. The second is our own local pipeline: a chain of small open models running free on the laptop. The goal was for the chain to beat Claude by 10 points at zero cost per photo. It didn't: Claude got the brand right about 39% of the time, our best chain 23%. The reason was the data, not the models.

1. The big picture: one generalist against an assembly line

Baseline

Claude (claude-opus-5-5)

Photo plus a prompt in, JSON out: one row per bottle with brand, product, size, fill and a box. It's a vision-language model (VLM), meaning a language model that can also read images (section 3 explains how).

  • Has seen most of the internet, so it knows premium spirits.
  • Costs money on every photo, takes seconds, and guesses: 144 of its 414 IDs came from shape, not from reading the label, and it invents brands it can't read (it called a Thai Dragon bottle "Flying Goose").
  • We ran it 5 times on each of the 7 photos, because it gives a slightly different answer each time (recall 0.595 ± 0.015).
Challenger

The local pipeline

Eight specialist steps, all open models, running on the Mac's GPU through Apple's MPS backend, with no API calls.

  • Each step is small, does one job and can be measured on its own.
  • Free per photo, and it can answer "unknown" instead of inventing something.
  • It only knows what's in the catalogue we built, so the data sets the ceiling.
Two test sets. The kitchen set is 7 photos of the same 12 items (spirits, sauces, milk, syrups) shot in Allen's place, which is what most of the leaderboard uses. The bar test set is 82 real bar photos from the web (DuckDuckGo and Wikimedia Commons, with licences, tiered easy/medium/hard), labelled only by humans. The official objective was set on the bar set, but most numbers below are from the kitchen set plus a 5-photo bar comparison.

3. How a model "sees": images as numbers

Every model here starts the same way. A photo is a grid of pixels, and each pixel is 3 numbers (red, green, blue, each 0 to 255). The grid gets chopped into small squares called patches, and each patch becomes a list of numbers.

Patches

OWLv2 looks at a 960 × 960 image in 16 × 16 pixel patches. 960 ÷ 16 = 60, so the image becomes 60 × 60 = 3,600 patches. Each patch is 16 × 16 × 3 = 768 raw numbers, squashed by a learned layer into one vector. The model then reads those 3,600 vectors the way a language model reads 3,600 words.

That's why we tile the photo (section 4). In a 4,000-pixel bar photo shrunk to 960, a small bottle might cover 2 or 3 patches, which isn't enough to see anything.

Hover a patch: each square becomes one vector

Attention: how patches talk to each other DEEP

Inside a transformer (the architecture under Claude, DINOv2, SigLIP2, OWLv2 and SAM), every patch asks every other patch "are you relevant to me?" and mixes in their information in proportion to the answer.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q KT√d ) V
Each patch makes three vectors: a query Q ("what am I looking for?"), a key K ("what do I contain?") and a value V ("what I'll pass on"). Multiply a query by every key to get a relevance score. Divide by √d so the numbers don't blow up when vectors are long. Softmax turns the scores into weights that add up to 1 (section 6 has a slider). Each patch's new vector is the weighted average of everyone's values. Stack this 12 to 40 times and a patch on a label "knows" it's part of a bottle on a shelf.
This is why one model can do everything: attention is general. It's also why bigger images cost more. 3,600 patches means 3,600 × 3,600 ≈ 13 million pairwise scores per layer.

4. Finding the bottles: detection maths

OWLv2 (Google) is an open-vocabulary detector. You give it text ("a bottle", "a jug", "a sauce bottle") and it scores every patch on "does a box here match this text?". It needed no training on our bottles. Any box scoring above 0.12 is kept.

Tiling with overlap

tile width = WT − (T − 1) · overlap = W3 − 2 × 0.4 = W2.2
Cut the photo into a 3 × 3 grid (T = 3) where neighbouring tiles overlap by 40%, so any bottle sits fully inside at least one tile. Each tile is enlarged to 960 pixels, so small bottles get more patches.
The photo is resized to 2,400 px wide. Each tile is 2,400 ÷ 2.2 ≈ 1,091 px, nearly half the photo instead of all of it. A bottle 40 px wide is 40 × 960/2400 = 16 px (one patch) without tiling, and 40 × 960/1091 ≈ 35 px (two patches) with it. Any box within 4 px of an inner tile edge is thrown away, because it's probably a half-bottle cut by the tile; a neighbouring tile sees it whole.

IoU and non-maximum suppression (NMS)

Overlapping tiles mean the same bottle gets found two or three times. To decide whether two boxes are the same bottle, we measure how much they overlap.

IoU(A, B) = area(A ∩ B)area(A ∪ B) = overlaparea A + area B − overlap
Intersection over union: the shared area divided by the total area covered. 1 means identical boxes, 0 means not touching. NMS: sort the boxes by score, keep the best, delete any other box with IoU ≥ 0.3 against it (or with 70% of itself inside it), then repeat.
Two 100 × 200 px boxes shifted 40 px sideways. Overlap = 60 × 200 = 12,000. Union = 20,000 + 20,000 − 12,000 = 28,000. IoU = 12,000 / 28,000 = 0.43, which is ≥ 0.3, so it's treated as the same bottle and the weaker box goes. Drag the slider: once the shift passes about 54 px, IoU drops below 0.3 and both boxes survive, i.e. two bottles standing next to each other.

Segmentation instead of boxes (SAM 3)

A box is 4 numbers. A segmentation mask is a yes/no for every pixel: is this pixel part of the bottle? Meta's SAM (Segment Anything) does this. SAM 2.1 needs a box as a hint; SAM 3 takes text, so one "bottle" pass over the whole photo gives every bottle's outline. For full bar walls Allen replaced OWLv2 with SAM 3, sorted bottles into shelf rows and then left to right, and dropped blobs covering several bottles. bar_03 went from at most 12 boxes to 113 bottles.

The crop check

fill ratio = mask pixelsbox pixels ≥ 0.55
If the bottle's outline covers less than 55% of its box, something's wrong: the bottle is hidden behind another, or the box is off. That crop is rejected and nobody estimates its fill. A shape check also compares height ÷ width with the median for that item and flags anything more than 25% off. On bar_03, 2 crops were rejected this way.

5. Embeddings: fingerprints in space

Your Embeddings.md note covers the idea. A backbone turns a crop into a vector, which is a point in space: 768 numbers for DINOv2, 768 for SigLIP2-base, 256 after our head. Photos of the same thing should land close together.

cos θ = a · b‖a‖ ‖b‖ = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + … + anbn√(a₁² + … + an²) · √(b₁² + … + bn²)
Multiply matching numbers, add them up, then divide by both lengths. The result is the cosine of the angle between the arrows: 1 = same direction (same thing), 0 = at right angles (unrelated), −1 = opposite. Length doesn't matter, only direction, so a bright and a dim photo of the same bottle can still match.
Three-number vectors. Crop a = (2, 1, 0), Jameson photo b = (4, 2, 1), Smirnoff photo c = (0, 1, 3).
a·b = 8 + 2 + 0 = 10, ‖a‖ = √5 = 2.24, ‖b‖ = √21 = 4.58 → cos = 10 / 10.25 = 0.98.
a·c = 0 + 1 + 0 = 1, ‖c‖ = √10 = 3.16 → cos = 1 / 7.07 = 0.14.
The nearest neighbour is Jameson. The real thing does exactly this with 768 numbers, against 42,652 gallery images, for every crop.
The trick that makes this fast: normalise every vector to length 1 in advance. Then cosine is just the dot product, and comparing one crop against 42,652 images is a single matrix multiplication that takes milliseconds.

Retrieval and the "unknown" threshold

prediction = { label of argmaxg cos(crop, g)  if max ≥ τ;  "unknown" otherwise }
argmax means "the item that gives the biggest value". Find the gallery image with the highest similarity and take its label. If even the best match is weak (below a threshold τ, which was 0.3 in the 12-item matcher), say "unknown". Clutter crops (a water bottle, a shaker, kitchen roll) are put in the gallery as their own class, so they have somewhere to go.
Retrieval means a new product just needs a few photos added to the gallery. No retraining. That's how most real retail-recognition systems work, and it's why the enrolment idea (shoot each bottle 3 times on a plain surface) is so strong.

6. Training maths: the only part we trained

The backbone stays frozen: its millions of weights never change. On top sits a small projection head that we did train: 768 → 1,024 → 256 numbers, with a GELU bend in the middle. Training a small layer on a frozen pretrained model is transfer learning. It's cheap enough for a laptop because we embed every training image once, save the vectors, and only push those saved vectors through the head 30 times.

Softmax and temperature

pi = esi / TΣj esj / T
Turns any list of scores into probabilities that add up to 1. Raise e to each score (always positive, and big scores get much bigger), then divide by the total. Temperature T sets how decisive it is: a small T exaggerates the gap, making the winner take nearly everything; a large T flattens everything towards equal. It's the same "temperature" setting LLMs have.

At T = 1 the answer is mushy (43% / 35% / 21%). At T = 0.1, which is what our training used, it's 88% / 12% / 0%. That sharpness is why the loss punishes near-misses hard.

Supervised contrastive loss (the training signal) DEEP

Li = − 1|P(i)| Σp ∈ P(i) log ezi·zp / TΣa ≠ i ezi·za / T
Take one photo i in a batch of 512. P(i) is every other photo in the batch with the same brand (the positives). For each positive, run a softmax over "how similar is i to everything else in the batch" and look up the probability given to that positive. We want it near 1, so the loss is −log of it (−log 1 = 0, no penalty; −log 0.01 = 4.6, big penalty). Average over the positives and over the batch. Then adjust the head's weights slightly so the loss goes down (gradient descent, using the AdamW optimiser, learning rate 0.001, decaying on a cosine schedule). That's 30 passes over the data (epochs).
One Jameson photo with one other Jameson in the batch. If the head maps them to similarity 0.8 and the closest Smirnoff to 0.6, then at T = 0.1 that's e8 against e6: the softmax gives Jameson 88%, loss = −log 0.88 = 0.13. If they were the other way round, the loss would be −log 0.12 = 2.1. The gradient nudges the weights so Jameson photos rotate towards each other in the 256-dimensional space. That's the animation in section 5.

The brand classifier: cross-entropy

L = − log pcorrect brand
A second output layer predicts one of the brands that have at least 30 training photos, through a softmax. The loss is how surprised it was by the right answer. It's trained alongside the contrastive loss, and the two are added.
The honest result: the head barely helped (brand recall 0.039 → 0.078). Training photos were clean product shots, test photos were dim shelves. The head learned what "close" means for packshots, and that didn't carry over. This is called domain shift, and it's the whole story of the day. Hyperparameters were tuned on a validation split (10% of products held out), never on the test set.

7. Zero-shot, OCR and fusion

Zero-shot: matching a picture to a sentence

SigLIP2 embeds images and text into the same space. So you can embed the sentence "a photo of a bottle of Jameson" and take its cosine with the crop, with no training on Jameson at all. That's zero-shot classification, the idea behind CLIP.

CLIP: softmax over the whole batch   |   SigLIP: σ(t · cos + b) per pair, σ(x) = 11 + e−x
DEEP CLIP learns by asking "which of these 32,000 captions belongs to this image?", which is one big softmax. SigLIP asks each image-caption pair separately, "match or not?", with a sigmoid that squeezes any number into 0 to 1. Same goal, but it scales better and gives calibrated per-pair scores.
We ran SigLIP2-so400m against 180 generic product names. Loose recall was 0.524, which looks great, but strict brand recall was 0.078. It was matching "Tomato Ketchup" and "Whole Milk", not brands. Always check which metric you're reading.

OCR: just read the label

Apple Vision reads text off each crop on the Mac itself, with language correction off (brand names aren't dictionary words). The text is then matched to brand names with fuzzy matching, because OCR misreads letters.

partial_ratio(brand, text) ≥ 90  (brand words of 6+ letters)   ·   exact word match (shorter words)
Fuzzy ratios come from edit distance (Levenshtein): how many single-letter insertions, deletions or swaps turn one string into the other, rescaled to 0 to 100. "partial" means the best-matching window of the longer text. Short words like "Jim" need an exact hit, otherwise they'd match everything.
OCR reads "TANQUERAY L0NDON DRY GIN" (a zero instead of an O). Against "Tanqueray": the window "tanqueray" matches exactly, 100 ≥ 90, so it counts as a hit. "L0NDON" vs "London" is one substitution in 6 letters, ratio about 83, so "London Dry" alone wouldn't pass. That's deliberate, because lots of gins say London Dry.
Biggest single lift of the day: brand recall 0.039 → 0.169 just by adding OCR. When the label is legible, reading beats recognising.

Fusion with z-scores

z = x − meanstandard deviation     scorebrand = 1.0 · zimage + 1.0 · zname  (OCR overrides if it names a brand)
Image similarity and text similarity are on different scales, so adding them raw would let one drown the other. A z-score asks "how many standard deviations above average is this brand, for this crop?". Once both signals are on the same scale, add them with equal weights. We left the weights untuned on purpose, because tuning them on the test photos would be cheating.
For one crop, across 4 brands, image similarities are 0.62, 0.55, 0.54, 0.53 (mean 0.56, sd 0.04) and name similarities are 0.10, 0.18, 0.09, 0.07 (mean 0.11, sd 0.04).
Brand 1: zimg = +1.5, zname = −0.25 → 1.25. Brand 2: −0.25 + 1.75 → 1.5. So brand 2 wins on a strong name signal even though brand 1 looked closest. Then the product is picked within the winning brand.

8. Fill level maths: geometry, not guessing

Asking Claude "how many ml are left?" was off by 19 percentage points on measured pours. So we did geometry, and only asked a model where the liquid line is.

Volume from a silhouette (the disc method)

V(below y) = Σrows r = y … base π (wr / 2)²   ⟹   fill % = Σline→base wr²Σneck→base wr²
A bottle is round, so each pixel row of its outline is a thin disc whose diameter is the outline's width there. A disc's area is π × radius², so a row twice as wide holds 4× as much. Add up the discs from the liquid line to the base, then divide by the total up to the neck. π and the ¼ cancel, so you only need the sum of width². No real-world units needed: it's a ratio.
Drag the slider. With the line halfway up the height, the bottle is 56% full by volume, and three-quarters of the way up it's already 83%, because the bottom is the fat part. That's why "fill % is volume, not height" is a rule in the repo. Only then is ml = fill % × size (e.g. 70% × 700 ml = 490 ml).

Where is "full"?

Bottles are filled to about the bottom of the neck. The code finds the shoulder: going up from mid-height, measure how fast the width shrinks over windows of 3% of the height. The steepest narrowing is the shoulder; the first row above it where the width shrinks by less than 10% per window is the neck. That row is 100%. This is a numerical derivative: rate of change of width with height.
0%

Camera tilt: why the base is an ellipse DEEP

sin θ = ellipse heightellipse width = 2 × (lowest pixel − widest row)base width
A circle seen from straight on is a line, and seen from above it's a circle. In between, it's an ellipse whose height-to-width ratio is the sine of the camera's angle above horizontal. The bottle's widest row near the bottom is the middle of the base ellipse; the lowest pixel is its front edge. Twice that gap is the ellipse height. So the photo tells you how high the camera was. The same logic says the liquid surface's true height is where it meets the side walls, not its front edge.
Base 80 px wide, lowest pixel 10 px below the widest row. Ellipse height = 20 px, sin θ = 20/80 = 0.25, θ = arcsin 0.25 = 14.5°, a camera slightly above shelf height. If the formula ever says more than 60°, the base is probably hidden, so the code falls back to the lowest pixel.

Is the liquid line real? Colour distance in Lab

ΔE = √( (L₁ − L₂)² + (a₁ − a₂)² + (b₁ − b₂)² )    must be ≥ 15
Lab is a colour space built so that equal distances look equally different to a human eye: L = lightness, a = green↔red, b = blue↔yellow. RGB doesn't work that way. Measure the average colour in a band just above the line and just below it, inside the bottle. Their straight-line distance is ΔE (Pythagoras in 3D). If it's under 15, there's no real edge there, so answer "unsure" rather than give a number.
Whisky below (L 45, a 18, b 40) against empty glass above (L 60, a 2, b 10): ΔE = √(225 + 256 + 900) = √1381 ≈ 37, a clear line. The threshold of 15 was chosen because the two clearly wrong readings had contrast 11 to 12 and every correct one had 21 or more. Caveat: it doesn't catch a sharp edge in the wrong place, like a printed orange band on a label (No. 18 read as 34 ml when other photos said about 320).

MAE = 1n Σ | predicted % − true % |
Mean absolute error in percentage points (pp): the average size of the miss, ignoring direction. "True" came from measured pours (7.5 and 15 ml into 50 ml miniatures, measured, never generated by a model), plus sealed full bottles.

9. Scoring maths: how we knew who won

Matching predictions to the truth (the Hungarian algorithm)

A system says "Jameson Irish Whiskey, Smirnoff Red, Heinz Ketchup…" and the truth is a list of 12 items. Which prediction goes with which item? Build a table of fuzzy text similarity (token_set_ratio, 0 to 100) between every prediction and every item, then find the one-to-one pairing with the highest total, so two predictions can't both claim the same bottle. That's the assignment problem, solved by the Hungarian algorithm (linear_sum_assignment). Pairs scoring below 60 count as wrong.
recall = items correctly matcheditems really there    precision = correct predictionsall predictions    count error = predicted count − true count
12 bottles in the photo. The system outputs 10, of which 7 match. Recall = 7/12 = 0.58 (it missed 5). Precision = 7/10 = 0.70 (3 answers were wrong). Count error = 10 − 12 = −2. Claude's −0.171 means it finds almost exactly the right number of bottles; the catalogue pipeline's +9 means it found clutter too.
Strict brand recall, the headline number: a match only counts if the predicted brand also scores ≥ 80 against the true brand, and "Unknown" or a generic name ("Coffee Liqueur") always fails. bar_recall counts only spirits, liqueurs, wine, beer and mixers, because nobody needs a bar camera for ketchup.

Variance across runs

Claude gives different answers each run, so we ran it 5 times. 0.595 ± 0.015 is the mean and standard deviation (the typical distance of a run from the mean). The catalogue pipeline and SigLIP2 give the same answer every time, so one run is enough. The open VLMs sample with temperature 0.7, so they got 3 runs.

Wilson confidence interval (the Judge tab) DEEP

p̂ ± margin,   centre = p̂ + z²/2n1 + z²/n,   margin = z √( p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/4n² )1 + z²/n,   z = 1.96
If a human judge says one system was right 8 times out of 20, the true rate isn't exactly 40%. With so few samples it could easily be 25% or 60%. A confidence interval gives the range you can believe 95% of the time (that's the 1.96). The textbook ±1.96·√(p(1−p)/n) breaks with small n or rates near 0 or 1 (it can give negative ranges). Wilson's version pulls the centre slightly towards 50% and behaves.

Perceptual hashes: keeping the test set out of training

Hamming(h₁, h₂) = number of the 64 bits that differ    drop if ≤ 6
A perceptual hash shrinks an image to a tiny greyscale thumbnail, takes the low-frequency pattern (via a DCT, the maths behind JPEG) and records 64 bits: brighter or darker than the median. Resized, recompressed or slightly cropped copies get nearly the same 64 bits. Any training image within 6 bits of a test photo is dropped, so a web photo that is also in our bar test set can't be memorised.

10. Data: why we lost

75 GB
pulled in one afternoon: Open Food Facts (alcohol products), retailer catalogues (Quebec's SAQ, Sweden's Systembolaget, Drink Finder), RP2K (Chinese retail shelves), WineSensed, wine-images-126k, wine and beer label sets, a whisky set, ImageNet and Open Images bottle crops, US TTB label filings, and web photos of the top ~300 bar brands (from trade rankings, never from our answers).
1,674 : 0
photos of Coca-Cola against shop photos of The Macallan. Open datasets are supermarket data. Premium spirits on a dim back bar barely exist in them.
72%
of web "photos of brand X" actually showed X. OCR verification (the fuzzy match in section 7): keep the photo only if its own label text names the brand. That gave 12.7k verified spirit photos free, with no human or Claude labelling. The gallery grew from 29,990 to 42,652 images.

Rules that kept the result honest

  • Ground truth only ever scores. Human labels never become an input: not a reference library, not a stock list in a prompt, not a guide to which images to download. Even the 12 retailer packshots were thrown out, because we'd picked them by looking at the answer list.
  • No distillation: Claude wasn't allowed to label our training data. Otherwise we'd be copying the system we were trying to beat, mistakes included.
  • Test photos deduplicated by perceptual hash (section 9). Thresholds tuned on a validation split of external data only.
  • Fill truth is measured (poured or weighed), never generated by a model.
The 98% that wasn't. Early on, the local pipeline hit 97.6% item recall. Its reference crops came from the other photos of the same 12 bottles: same room, same light, minutes apart. That's data leakage: the answers leaked into the inputs. The method is called leave-one-photo-out (LOPO): hold one photo back, use the other 6 as references. It's valid for testing the matcher and invalid as a result. The honest equivalent, retailer packshots only, scored 59% crop accuracy. It's in the leaderboard marked follows_rule = no.
LOPO numbers, for the record (invalid as results): 1 reference crop per item → 0.81 crop accuracy / 0.88 item recall; 2 → 0.85 / 0.93; 3 → 0.87 / 0.98. What it does show: with 3 photos of each bottle in the actual room, retrieval nearly solves identification. That's the case for an enrolment step at each bar.

Last hour: synthetic data

If the photos don't exist, render them. src/synth/render.py takes flat label artwork from US government filings (TTB COLA), wraps it onto a generic bottle shape picked by spirit type in Blender (3D software, running headless), stands it on a shelf with distractor bottles, and randomises the lighting (an HDRI: a 360° photo of a real room used as the light source), camera angle, fill level, glass tint and depth of field. Half the filings were back labels, so they were filtered on the words "Government Warning". Randomising everything is called domain randomisation: if training covers enough variety, a real bar is just one more variation. We stopped before evaluating it, so whether it helps is unknown.

11. Running open VLMs on the laptop

We also asked "what if Claude's role was played by a free open model?" Same prompt and schema, run locally with Apple's MLX library.

memory ≈ parameters × bits per weight ÷ 8
A model is billions of numbers (parameters). Normally each is 16 bits. Quantisation stores them in 4 or 8 bits, which loses a little accuracy and saves most of the memory and time.
Qwen3.5-9B at 4-bit: 9 × 10⁹ × 4 ÷ 8 = 4.5 GB, so it fits easily, at 59 s per photo. Qwen3-VL-32B at 4-bit: 16 GB, 242 s per photo, and it scored no better (brand 0.156 against 0.203). Bigger isn't automatically better; newer training beat size. Gemma 4 12B mostly answered "unknown" (0.069). Qwen3-VL-8B sometimes got stuck in a loop and listed 52 bottles.

13. The app, the speed work, and who did what

The web app (src/api.py, dashboard.py)

  • Identify: upload a photo, get bottles, boxes and fill. pipeline=local (SAM 3 finds everything) or pipeline=reference (the 12-item matcher).
  • Testing tab: the leaderboard by brand recall, Claude's runs, and a per-photo inspector.
  • Data tab: browse the training images and their metadata, filtered by source and brand.
  • Judge tab: blind A/B. A human sees two answers for the same bottle without knowing which system gave which, picks the right one, and the tab shows live accuracy with Wilson intervals.
  • Demo: instant replay of bar_03 (scripted, see above).

Making it fast

  • The GPU can only run one model safely at a time, so SAM and crop checks run in order under a lock.
  • Claude fill calls are network waits, not GPU work, so they run in parallel in a thread pool, with OCR in its own thread. A cold run on IMG_6342 went from 27 to 37 s down to 10 to 11 s.
  • Caching: SAM masks are reused (the whole-photo outline feeds fill, with no second SAM call), and OCR results and gallery embeddings are saved to disk.

16. If someone asks

"Why not just use Claude?"

You could, and it wins today. But it costs money on every photo, a bar shooting its shelves daily adds up, and it confidently invents brands it can't read. A local chain is free, can say "unknown", and every step can be measured and improved on its own.

"So why did you lose?"

Data, not models. Claude has effectively seen the internet, premium spirits included. Open datasets are supermarket data. Our image side alone got about 16% brand recall; reading the label lifted it to 23%. Training on packshots and testing on dim shelves is domain shift, and it showed up everywhere, including the fill models trained on lab glassware.

"What would close the gap?"

Real photos of the bottles bars actually stock. Cheapest: an enrolment shot of each bottle at each bar (3 photos on a plain surface), which our invalid-but-telling LOPO run says gets close to 90%+. Or synthetic renders from label artwork, built but not yet evaluated. Then fine-tune the backbone itself, not just the head.

"How do you know your numbers aren't fake?"

Test labels only score; near-duplicates of test photos are removed from training by perceptual hash; thresholds are tuned elsewhere; no Claude labels in training; fill truth is measured. The one time we broke this (98%), it was logged as invalid.

"What's the clever bit on fill?"

Don't ask a model for a number. Outline the bottle, sum the width² of every pixel row to get volume, correct for camera tilt from the base ellipse, and only ask the model which numbered tick the liquid sits at. Refuse when the colour edge is weak. Error went from 19 points to about 2, on very few measured pours.

"Embeddings? Contrastive? Explain like I'm five."

Every photo becomes a dot on a giant map. Photos of the same thing should be neighbours. Identifying a bottle means finding its nearest neighbour on the map. Training means moving dots so same-brand photos huddle together and different brands spread apart.
